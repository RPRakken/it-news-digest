Faster protection, fewer interruptions: How Microsoft Edge is rethinking updates
blogs.windows.com • Oct 15, 2026

Faster protection, fewer interruptions: How Microsoft Edge is rethinking updates At a glance Microsoft Edge is developing new ways to help security updates reach people faster with less disruption. A seamless-updates prototype shows how Edge could update while work continues. Available today, Restart to update on idle (formerly Instant Updates) can apply pending updates when devices are idle and Security Update Alerts help organizations identify devices that need critical updates. Imagine an update that does not interrupt your work As AI speeds up both the discovery of security flaws and the pace of attacks, getting protection to everyone quickly matters more than ever. Security updates are essential. Interruptions shouldn’t have to be. That’s why we’ve developed a working prototype that moves the browser to a new, more secure version while your open work continues, with no restart prompt and hardly anything to notice. Imagine you are halfway through a long form, such as an expense report. You’ve found the receipts and entered all the details. Then you notice a security update is ready…but you’re not. Restart now, or risk losing your work? It’s easy to see why people hesitate to take updates. With seamless updates, instead of asking you to pause and relaunch the browser, Edge applies the new version in the background. The browser, its supporting processes, and your open tabs transition to that version while the form stays open and your work remains in place. In this demo, we clicked the Update button so you can see the moment the browser begins updating. Our goal is that you won’t have to click the update button at all; you would simply continue working with a safer browser. Importantly, your updates could be applied within minutes of availability. There’s a reason browser updates have traditionally relied on a restart. A browser needs to coordinate many connected processes, all of which must transition safely and reliably to the new version while preserving what you are doing. We’re sharing this prototype early in development to make the ambition tangible and help advance the broader conversation about how browsers can move to more secure versions without interrupting people’s work. As such, it is not in production code or part of Chromium today. We invite others across the industry to join us in exploring new approaches to getting critical protection to people faster and with less disruption. Restart to update on idle: A practical step you can take today You don’t need to wait for seamless updates to arrive to reduce update friction. You can opt in to Restart to update on idle (formerly named “Instant Updates”), which relaunches Edge when the device is idle, applies a pending update, and restores open tabs. Organizations can configure the experience through the Monitoring dashboard in Edge management service. This is different from the seamless-updates prototype: a restart still occurs, but it shifts that interruption away from active work and helps important security fixes reach everyone sooner. If you typically wait to update your browser, Restart to update on idle can help you get the latest protection in hours instead of days. Why faster updates matter A security update can only protect you after apply it. The time between a fix becoming available and the update being applied is known in security circles as the patch gap. Restarts add friction to that gap: people may keep a browser open for days because they are in the middle of something important. That window matters even more as AI changes the speed and scale of cybersecurity. The 2026 Microsoft Digital Defense Report describes how AI is compressing attack timelines and accelerating vulnerability discovery. Defenders need to find and fix issues quickly—and help those fixes reach people just as quickly. When protection can’t wait, Edge can move faster Microsoft Edge is built on Chromium and benefits from the security work of the broader open-source community.

Python 3.15 delivers a native-by-default experience on Windows on Arm
blogs.windows.com • Oct 14, 2026

Windows on Arm has been ready for Python development for some time. With Python 3.15, the broader ecosystem has matured significantly, with more package maintainers publishing native Arm64 wheels, allowing Windows on Arm to support mainstream Python development across a broad and growing set of workloads. Windows on Arm is now a Tier 2 CPython platform, backed by continuous testing and CPython’s release-quality commitments. That readiness extends beyond the runtime. Widely used packages such as NumPy, SciPy, pandas, scikit-learn, Django, and FastAPI are available, while Jupyter, VS Code, Ruff, and pytest support everyday development workflows. GitHub-hosted Windows on Arm runners also let open-source maintainers build and test their projects natively. This did not happen with a single release. Native Windows on Arm builds of CPython have been available for several versions. What changes with Python 3.15 is the maturity of the experience. Windows on Arm is now a regular CPython target; the native runtime is selected by default on Windows on Arm devices, and CPython’s Tier 2 requirements call for platform failures to be fixed or the responsible changes reverted. Package maintainers now have practical infrastructure for native Windows on Arm validation, and many widely used projects publish native Arm64 wheels. Windows on Arm is now a practical platform for many mainstream Python development scenarios. As of September 2026, a scan of the 1,000 most-downloaded PyPI projects shows about 94% of the projects’ latest releases published either a native Windows on Arm wheel or a platform-independent wheel. This measures published wheel availability, not end-to-end application compatibility. Anaconda’s main channel also includes more than 2,500 packages published for Windows on Arm. Platform readiness does not mean every Python application is automatically ready for Windows on Arm. The runtime and a broad set of mainstream development workflows are established, but application readiness still depends on the complete dependency graph, particularly when packages rely on native extensions. Developers should confirm native Arm support for their dependencies during migration and testing. A native Python experience by default Windows on Arm is a PEP 11 Tier 2 platform. This designation reflects a strong support commitment; the platform has reliable continuous integration and active core developers, and platform-breaking changes must be addressed promptly. Windows on Arm CI results are considered as part of CPython’s release process. This is a clear measure of how far Windows on Arm support has progressed. Installation is now simpler. With Python Install Manager 26.4, Windows on Arm devices default to the Arm64 platform, so requesting Python 3.15 selects the native Arm64 runtime unless configuration or an explicit architecture tag overrides it. Python 3.15 ships a complete set of Windows on Arm component packages alongside the x64 packages. CPython runs tests on Windows on Arm, including the standard interpreter and free-threaded configuration. These jobs run as part of the normal Windows build matrix. You can confirm the architecture of the running interpreter by launching Python and inspecting its startup banner. A native Windows on Arm interpreter includes 64-bit (ARM64) in the compiler information; an x64 interpreter includes 64-bit (AMD64). Running Python natively keeps the interpreter and native package dependencies on the same Arm64 architecture. Applications that still depend on x64 packages can continue to run through Windows emulation, providing a practical transition path while those dependencies add native Arm support. Building a cloud-native foundation for AI The relationship between model innovation and AI infrastructure has never been more clear; as AI becomes more capable, the platform underneath it matters even more. Agents are fundamentally changing how applications work, and with that, what organizations need from the cloud. Cloud-native practices gave organizations the scale, flexibility and resilience to build a generation of distributed applications. Agents build on that foundation, but they work differently, placing new demands on it. Unlike applications designed around more predictable workflows, agents behave in entirely new ways, gathering information and deep intelligence from across the business, using tools, making decisions, and coordinating work across long-running processes. To complete complex tasks, they pause for approval, respond to new information in real time and change course as conditions evolve. All this changes what the platform needs to deliver. Customers need infrastructure that adapts as demand changes, applications that remain reliable as work becomes more dynamic, and the visibility and control to understand what is happening across the system. Meeting those needs doesn’t mean starting over, but it does require that the cloud-native foundation organizations already rely on, to evolve. Compute, storage and networking need to work together. Application platforms need to make it easier to build and run AI workloads. Open technologies need to give customers flexibility without adding unnecessary complexity. The platform decisions organizations make today are increasingly strategic, shaping how effectively they can put AI to work for their business. Evolving the cloud-native foundation is essential to helping organizations get more from AI today while giving them the flexibility to take advantage of new capabilities as they emerge. Optimize infrastructure for AI workloads The true measure of AI infrastructure is the useful work it enables. AI workloads rarely place the same demands on infrastructure from beginning to end. Different parts of the work may need accelerated compute, CPUs, fast access to data or connectivity across applications and environments. The answer isn’t simply more capacity, but rather, to match the right resources to the right work at the right time. That makes coordination across the infrastructure extremely important. Compute, storage and networking need to work together as demands change. When they do, teams can spend less time managing infrastructure and more time focused on the applications and outcomes that matter to their business. Economics matter, too. Infrastructure needs to be tuned to the demands of the application and the business value it delivers, with the right balance of performance, reliability and cost across the complete workload. Basecamp Research is one example. The company used Azure to assemble 9.7 trillion nucleotides for EDEN, its therapeutic-design models, and reported laboratory validation across three therapeutic applications within about three weeks. In this example, AI didn’t simply require more infrastructure, it needed AI infrastructure that was coordinated and designed around the outcome – bringing together the compute, data and platform capabilities needed to move complex work forward. As Glen Gowers, co-founder of Basecamp Research put it: “To really be successful here, we need those three ingredients to come together. We need someone scaling the data; that’s us. But someone who really knows how to scale models and scale compute is Microsoft.” Leadership takeaway: Measure success by the useful work AI completes. Judge infrastructure investment by the performance, reliability and cost of delivering those outcomes, with compute, storage and networking operating as one system. Advancing hardware security with open-source Integrated HSM technology
azure.microsoft.com • Oct 13, 2026

At this year’s OCP Global Summit, Microsoft announced Project Tessera, the evolution of the Azure Integrated Hardware Security Module (HSM), into a fully open-source initiative developed in collaboration with Marvell®, Oracle, and Open Compute Project (OCP). Project Tessera advances our broader commitment to open, industrywide standards for platform security. The motivation behind Project Tessera is straightforward: the entire industry benefits when we collaborate and advance hardware security together. Project Tessera carries forward the workload directly attached to the HSM architecture introduced by Azure Integrated HSM, making the architecture, specifications, protocols, drivers, software, firmware, and reproducible build environments open source for the broader community. Over time, the initiative will advance toward a fully open-source silicon implementation, while making hardware broadly available, enabling organizations across the ecosystem to adopt, integrate, and contribute to the platform. This approach builds on the model Microsoft established with other industry collaborators on Caliptra, a fully open-source silicon root-of-trust that has since been broadly adopted across the hardware ecosystem. Through Project Tessera, industry leaders including Microsoft, Marvell®, Oracle, and the Open Compute Project will apply that same model of transparency and collaboration to advance hardware key protection with the industry. The cybersecurity landscape is rapidly changing, and protecting keys and credentials has never been more important. We are now facing two generational transformations that are fundamentally reshaping how we view the cybersecurity landscape: - AI is increasing the speed and scale of cyberattacks AI-orchestrated attacks are increasing the scale, speed, and sophistication of attacks, giving adversaries new ways to operate and compressing the time between vulnerability discovery and exploitation. These capabilities amplify risks such as credential theft, key exfiltration, and signing abuse. Keys and credentials are high-value control assets. When compromised, they can enable attackers to impersonate trusted entities, bypass security controls, authorize malicious operations, expose sensitive data, and undermine software security defenses. Protecting these assets requires infrastructure with layered, hardened protections capable of withstanding sustained attacks across multiple concurrent attack vectors. - Quantum computing is changing the cryptographic risk horizon Quantum computing promises transformative advances, but it also presents a risk to many of today’s cryptographic algorithms. A sufficiently powerful quantum computer could break widely used public-key cryptography, undermining digital signatures, authentication, and identity verification. Post-quantum cryptography was once viewed as an important but distant technology shift. Advances in quantum computing have changed that perspective as organizations recognize the urgency, scale, and complexity of the transition. HSM-backed signing infrastructure will be among the first critical systems to migrate, requiring HSMs that support post-quantum cryptography and are interoperable with classical HSMs to support migration. These emerging threats motivated the development of Azure Integrated HSM, which was designed to meet rigorous security standards for hardware-based key protection, while also accelerating Microsoft’s transition to post-quantum cryptography through scale deployments of quantum resilient integrated HSMs. Azure Integrated HSM introduced a workload-attached HSM that performs cryptographic operations closer to the workload while keys remain continuously protected within the HSM security boundary.

Energy-efficient inference through software-defined dataflow Maia 200 introduced a new class of accelerator architecture called Software Defined Locally Accessed Dataflow Architecture, or SDLA. It begins with the observation that efficient data management, including storing, moving and converting data, is one of the central challenges in modern AI systems. The Maia programmable dataflow architecture separates control path programming from data path execution and allows software to explicitly coordinate data placement, movement, conversion and synchronization across specialized local memories, DMA engines, compute engines, and the network. Maia 300 evolves this architecture for long-context, reasoning-intensive, and decode-heavy inference. First, memory moves from the uniform memory access (UMA) organization of the SoC to a locality-aware non-uniform (NUMA) organization across chiplets, so software can place data near the compute that uses it. Second, new narrow-precision formats with hardware-accelerated data type conversion reduce the bytes moved for each token. Third, the rack doubles to 96 accelerators and the scale-up domain grows to a superpod of 12,288 accelerators, enabling more accelerators to work effectively as a single system. Rather than scaling performance through increased power and silicon area alone, Maia 300 improves performance per watt through a set of architectural innovations that optimize data movement, locality, precision, and memory efficiency across the system: - Multi-chiplet architecture boosts performance by placing compute on TSMC N2P, where the advanced process delivers the greatest performance-per-watt benefit, while separating memory-controller and I/O functions onto dies optimized for bandwidth, connectivity, and cost. This increases the silicon area dedicated to compute performance and allows each function to use the technology best suited to its role, while SDLA manages locality and data movement across the package. This strategy increases the system’s compute FLOPs by 36%, adds 2.5 times HBM bandwidth, and provides 14% more scale-up bandwidth. - Optimized low precision inference, introducing enhanced MXFP4 and sub-3-bit storage formats.

"Effectively, the weights would become part of the malware payload." The idea, at a high level, explains how AI models could potentially break into internet-connected devices and use their computing power to run themselves and spread to other devices, without having to rely on expensive data centers all the time. A more powerful model that's too large to fit into a single device could be split into small chunks and run across multiple machines to collectively run it. A threat actor could build a distributed LLM botnet from poorly secured IoT devices, making it powerful enough to run a capable model. Some of the ways an AI worm could propagate include - - Small models that replicate through a compromise of host machines on which they run, also called weight-based replicators. IoT devices make for a lucrative target because of their minimal security controls. - Target datacenters and other high-compute targets to set up unmonitored rogue deployments, also classified as frontier replicators or open-weight replicators based on the model used. - Steal an AI service provider's API keys or platform credentials to run copies of itself, also called API-based replicators. In this case, the provider could be deceived into thinking it is serving ordinary API requests from a legitimate customer, without realizing that a malicious or self-replicating system is behind them. However, they are also likely to detect more as unusual API usage could trigger security alerts, leading to the credentials being revoked or the accounts getting blocked. It also revealed that an AI IoT worm powered by inexpensive Android TV boxes could be a "slow, distributed" system that can produce a meaningful amount of AI computation when a fleet comprising hundreds of thousands of such devices is networked together to run an inference engine. Alternatively, it could take the form of a potent cyber model capable of breaching data centers at a high pace, or a model that scams people for compute credits. "We don't yet have a full rogue replicator, but agents are becoming increasingly capable of completing the individual steps in the replication chain (and passing on their adaptations to future copies of themselves)," Anthropic said. "We expect AI botnets to infect more than IoT devices, and to use weight-based replication as just one strategy in their playbook. The actor moved through tenants in sorted GUID order, a few seconds apart, which points to a script working from a target list." In the observed attackers, the adversary has been observed authenticating to the app using their own tooling, in a manner analogous to the backup service, and obtains tokens with the app's existing permissions. Given that the app has extensive permissions to access customer data, an attacker in possession of the tokens can read mailboxes, files, and directory data the backup service can. Adding a credential to an MSP management application can extend access beyond the one tenant, because those applications are often consented into the MSP's customers' tenants. A credential on a security tool's application gives the holder whatever that tool is permitted to do." Todyl, in a similar analysis, said it detected an unauthorized party authenticating to Microsoft Graph as the ConnectWise Cloud Backup application.
